Going to the DMV with Autism or Sensory Sensitivities: What to Expect

The DMV is one of the hardest outings for a lot of neurodivergent people — long unpredictable waits, fluorescent lights, background noise, forms, and unfamiliar steps. If you're dreading it, you're not overreacting. Here's how to make it as calm as possible.

Last updated July 2026

Why the DMV is so tough

It's rarely one thing — it's the pile-up: bright overhead lighting, a crowded room, an unclear process, and open-ended waiting with no idea how long it'll take. For anyone sensitive to sensory input or uncertainty, that combination is a lot.

Prepare before you go

  • Book an appointment if you can. Many DMV and government offices let you schedule online — an appointment usually means far less waiting than a walk-in.
  • Go right at opening. First thing in the morning is almost always the shortest, quietest line.
  • Bring every document. Check the exact list online beforehand and bring it all. Nothing spikes stress like being turned away and having to return.
  • Pack your sensory tools. Headphones or earplugs for the noise, sunglasses or a cap for the lights, a fidget, water, and a snack for the wait.
  • Know the steps in advance. Usually it's: check in, take a number, wait, get called to a window, done. Walking through that ahead of time makes it far less overwhelming.

While you're there

  • Ask about accommodations. You can quietly ask staff if there's a shorter path, a quieter spot to wait, or help with the process. Many are willing if you ask.
  • Give yourself an exit valve. If waiting inside is too much, ask whether they'll text or call your number so you can wait outside or in the car.
  • Have a calm-down plan. Know a signal or a step (step outside, headphones on) you'll use if it gets to be too much — deciding it in advance takes the pressure off.

Know the specific office before you go

Not every DMV or government office is the same — some are calmer, better laid out, or less crowded than others.
